Mathematical Foundations of Multi-Series Probabilistic Forecasting (multi_prob_forecasts)

Empirical Error Covariance, Regularization, Multivariate Distributions, and Gaussian Copula

Peshbeen Package Mathematical Documentation

Introduction & Problem Formulation

In multi-series time-series forecasting (such as retail store-item demand, energy grid load across sub-stations, or traffic flow across sensor networks), multiple interdependent time series $\{y_{s, t}\}$ are observed across series identifiers $s \in \{1, 2, \dots, N\}$ over historical time steps $t \in \{1, 2, \dots, T\}$.

While point forecasting models (like ml_multi_forecaster) generate conditional expectation trajectories:

$$\mathbf{\hat{y}}_h = \left[ \hat{y}_{1, h}, \, \hat{y}_{2, h}, \, \dots, \, \hat{y}_{N, h} \right]^T \in \mathbb{R}^N \quad \text{for } h \in \{1, 2, \dots, H\}$$

real-world decision making (e.g. joint inventory management, portfolio risk, capacity planning) requires the joint predictive distribution:

$$P\left( \mathbf{Y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{Y}_H \mid \mathcal{F}_T \right)$$

where $\mathcal{F}_T$ denotes the historical filtration up to time $T$.

Because shocks in one series (e.g. promotional cannibalization, weather shocks, macro trends) are correlated with shocks in other series, modeling individual series variances independently underestimates system-wide tail risk and mischaracterizes aggregate uncertainty.

multi_prob_forecasts solves this by explicitly modeling the cross-series error covariance structure and coupling point forecasts with multivariate predictive distributions:

  1. Multivariate Normal ($\mathcal{N}_N$)
  2. Multivariate Student-$t$ ($t_N$)
  3. Gaussian Copula with Empirical Marginals
  4. Joint Non-Parametric Empirical Resampling

    5. Transformation Pipeline & In-Sample Residual Recovery

    Forward Target Transformations

    During data preparation (data_prep), each series $y_{s, t}$ undergoes up to five sequential transformations to stabilize variance and remove non-stationarity:

    1. Box-Cox Transformation (Variance stabilization):

      2. $$z_{s, t} = B(y_{s, t}; \lambda_s) = \begin{cases} \frac{(y_{s, t} + c_s)^{\lambda_s} - 1}{\lambda_s}, & \lambda_s \neq 0 \\ \ln(y_{s, t} + c_s), & \lambda_s = 0 \end{cases}$$

      1. Deterministic / Polynomial Trend Removal:

        2. $$w_{s, t} = z_{s, t} - \hat{\tau}_{s, t}$$

        1. Ordinary Differencing ($d_s \in \{1, 2\}$):

          2. $$\Delta^{d_s} w_{s, t} = (1 - L)^{d_s} w_{s, t}$$

          1. Seasonal Differencing ($m_s \ge 2$):

            2. $$\Delta_{m_s} \Delta^{d_s} w_{s, t} = (1 - L^{m_s}) \Delta^{d_s} w_{s, t}$$

            1. Target Scaling (e.g. StandardScaler):

              2. $$u_{s, t} = \frac{\Delta_{m_s} \Delta^{d_s} w_{s, t} - \mu_s}{\sigma_s}$$

              The machine learning model is trained to minimize the prediction loss on the transformed target space $u_{s, t}$.

              In-Sample Residual & Fitted Value Inversion

              Let $\hat{u}_{s, t} = f(\mathbf{x}_{s, t})$ be the raw prediction produced by the estimator in the transformed space.

              To obtain statistically meaningful residuals on the original measurement scale:

              1. Invert Target Scaling:

                2. $$\hat{\delta}_{s, t} = \text{scaler}_s^{-1}(\hat{u}_{s, t}) = \hat{u}_{s, t} \cdot \sigma_s + \mu_s$$

                $$\delta_{s, t} = \text{scaler}_s^{-1}(u_{s, t}) = u_{s, t} \cdot \sigma_s + \mu_s$$

                The 1-step prediction error in the unscaled differenced/detrended space is:

                $$e_{s, t} = \delta_{s, t} - \hat{\delta}_{s, t}$$

                1. Invariance of 1-Step Errors under Linear Differencing & Detrending:

                  2. For any linear difference $(1 - L) w_{s, t} = w_{s, t} - w_{s, t-1}$, the 1-step ahead prediction given true history $w_{s, t-1}$ is:

                  $$\hat{w}_{s, t} = w_{s, t-1} + \hat{\delta}_{s, t}$$

                  Subtracting $\hat{w}_{s, t}$ from the true observation $w_{s, t}$:

                  $$w_{s, t} - \hat{w}_{s, t} = (w_{s, t} - w_{s, t-1}) - \hat{\delta}_{s, t} = \delta_{s, t} - \hat{\delta}_{s, t} = e_{s, t}$$

                  The error in the pre-differenced / pre-detrended space is identically equal to $e_{s, t}$.

                  1. Box-Cox Inversion:
                  2. If Box-Cox was not applied:

                    3. $$\hat{y}_{s, t} = y_{s, t} - e_{s, t}, \quad \text{resid}_{s, t} = e_{s, t}$$

                    • If Box-Cox was applied:

                    $$\hat{z}_{s, t} = z_{s, t} - e_{s, t}$$

                    $$\hat{y}_{s, t} = B^{-1}(\hat{z}_{s, t}; \lambda_s), \quad \text{resid}_{s, t} = y_{s, t} - \hat{y}_{s, t}$$

                    Empirical Error Covariance & Correlation Estimation

                    Joint Error Matrix Construction

                    Let $M$ be the number of aligned historical or cross-validation evaluation points common to all $N$ series. The joint error matrix $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ is:

                    $$\mathbf{E} = \begin{bmatrix}

                    e_{1, 1} & e_{2, 1} & \dots & e_{N, 1} \\

                    e_{1, 2} & e_{2, 2} & \dots & e_{N, 2} \\

                    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\

                    e_{1, M} & e_{2, M} & \dots & e_{N, M}

                    \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{e}_{(1)}^T \\ \mathbf{e}_{(2)}^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{e}_{(M)}^T \end{bmatrix}$$

                    where each column $\mathbf{e}_s \in \mathbb{R}^M$ contains the residual series for series $s$, and each row $\mathbf{e}_{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ represents the joint cross-series error vector at observation $t$.

                    Sample Covariance Matrix

                    The unbiased empirical sample covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{raw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is computed as:

                    $$\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{M - 1} \sum_{t=1}^M \left( \mathbf{e}_{(t)} - \mathbf{\bar{e}} \right) \left( \mathbf{e}_{(t)} - \mathbf{\bar{e}} \right)^T$$

                    where $\mathbf{\bar{e}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^M \mathbf{e}_{(t)}$ is the mean residual vector across all series.

                    Exact Symmetry and Spectral Positive Semi-Definite (PSD) Regularization

                    In empirical settings with finite samples or collinear series, numerical floating-point inaccuracies or rank deficiency can lead to negative eigenvalues $\lambda_i \le 0$, rendering the covariance matrix non-invertible and invalidating Cholesky decomposition.

                    To guarantee mathematical validity, multi_prob_forecasts applies spectral eigenvalue thresholding:

                    1. Symmetrization:

                      2. $$\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{sym}} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{raw}} + \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{raw}}^T \right)$$

                      1. Eigendecomposition:

                        2. $$\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{sym}} = \mathbf{V} \mathbf{\Lambda} \mathbf{V}^T$$

                        where $\mathbf{V} = [\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_N]$ is the orthonormal matrix of eigenvectors, and $\mathbf{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N)$ contains the real eigenvalues.

                        1. PSD Floor Projection:

                          2. We project all eigenvalues onto the strictly positive half-space with threshold $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$:

                          $$\lambda_i^{\text{clipped}} = \max(\lambda_i, \, \epsilon)$$

                          $$\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\text{psd}} = \text{diag}\left( \lambda_1^{\text{clipped}}, \, \dots, \, \lambda_N^{\text{clipped}} \right)$$

                          1. Reconstructed Covariance Matrix:

                            2. $$\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{V} \mathbf{\Lambda}_{\text{psd}} \mathbf{V}^T$$

                            By construction, $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ is strictly symmetric and positive definite ($\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{x} > 0 \, \forall \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$).

                            Empirical Correlation Matrix

                            From the regularized covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$, we extract the empirical standard deviation vector $\mathbf{s} = \left[ \sqrt{\Sigma_{1, 1}}, \, \dots, \, \sqrt{\Sigma_{N, N}} \right]^T$ and construct the diagonal scaling matrix $\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(\mathbf{s})$.

                            The cross-series empirical correlation matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is:

                            $$\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{D}^{-1} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{D}^{-1}$$

                            with elements:

                            $$R_{i, j} = \frac{\Sigma_{i, j}}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{i, i}} \sqrt{\Sigma_{j, j}}}, \quad R_{i, i} = 1.0, \quad R_{i, j} \in [-1, 1]$$

                            Multivariate Sampling Methods in .sample()

                            For a given forecast horizon $H$ and $B$ Monte Carlo simulated paths ($m = 1, \dots, B$), we generate joint trajectory matrices for each series:

                            $$\mathbf{Y}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H} \quad \text{for } s \in \{1, \dots, N\}$$

                            Let $\mathbf{\hat{y}}_s = \left[ \hat{y}_{s, 1}, \hat{y}_{s, 2}, \dots, \hat{y}_{s, H} \right]$ be the multi-step point forecast for series $s$.

                            $$\mathbf{Y}_{m, h, s} = \hat{y}_{s, h} + \epsilon_{m, h, s}$$

                            where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{m, h} = \left[ \epsilon_{m, h, 1}, \, \dots, \, \epsilon_{m, h, N} \right]^T \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is drawn from one of the four joint distributions below.

                            Method 1: Multivariate Normal Distribution (method="mvn")

                            Definition

                            Assumes joint Gaussian innovation errors across series at each forecast horizon step $h$:

                            $$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{m, h} \sim \mathcal{N}_N\left(\mathbf{0}, \, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)$$

                            Sampling Algorithm via Cholesky Decomposition

                            1. Compute the lower triangular Cholesky factor $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ such that:

                              2. $$\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{L}^T$$

                              1. Draw $N$ standard independent normal variates:

                                2. $$\mathbf{z}_{m, h} \sim \mathcal{N}_N\left(\mathbf{0}, \, \mathbf{I}_N\right)$$

                                1. Correlate and scale:

                                  2. $$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{m, h} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{z}_{m, h}$$

                                  Method 2: Multivariate Student-$t$ Distribution (method="student_t")

                                  Gaussian Scale Mixture Formulation

                                  A random vector $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ follows a multivariate Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom and dispersion matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$, denoted $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim t_N(\nu, \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{\Sigma})$, if it can be represented as a Gaussian scale mixture:

                                  $$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{m, h} = \frac{\mathbf{Z}_{m, h}}{\sqrt{\frac{W_{m, h}}{\nu}}}$$

                                  where:

                                  1. $\mathbf{Z}_{m, h} \sim \mathcal{N}_N\left(\mathbf{0}, \, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)$ is a zero-mean multivariate normal vector.
                                  2. $W_{m, h} \sim \chi^2(\nu)$ is an independent chi-squared scalar random variable with $\nu = \text{df\_t\_deg}$ degrees of freedom.

                                    3. Method 3: Gaussian Copula with Non-Parametric Marginals (method="copula")

                                    Mathematical Foundation: Sklar's Theorem

                                    Sklar's Theorem (1959) states that any multivariate joint cumulative distribution function $F(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_N)$ can be uniquely decomposed into its $N$ univariate marginal distributions $F_1, F_2, \dots, F_N$ and a copula function $C: [0, 1]^N \to [0, 1]$ that fully characterizes the dependency structure:

                                    $$F(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_N) = C\left( F_1(e_1), \, F_2(e_2), \, \dots, \, F_N(e_N) \right)$$

                                    For the Gaussian Copula, $C_{\mathbf{R}}$ is parameterized by the cross-series correlation matrix $\mathbf{R}$:

                                    $$C_{\mathbf{R}}(u_1, \dots, u_N) = \mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{R}}\left( \Phi^{-1}(u_1), \, \Phi^{-1}(u_2), \, \dots, \, \Phi^{-1}(u_N) \right)$$

                                    Step-by-Step Sampling Algorithm

                                    1. Step 1 — Correlated Gaussian Latent Variables:

                                      2. Draw latent vector $\mathbf{Z}_{m, h} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ from the multivariate normal distribution with empirical correlation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and unit variances:

                                      $$\mathbf{Z}_{m, h} \sim \mathcal{N}_N\left(\mathbf{0}, \, \mathbf{R}\right)$$

                                      1. Step 2 — Probability Integral Transform (PIT):

                                        2. Map each latent Gaussian variable $Z_{m, h, s}$ to a uniform variable $U_{m, h, s} \in (0, 1)$ using the univariate standard Gaussian CDF $\Phi$:

                                        $$U_{m, h, s} = \Phi\left( Z_{m, h, s} \right)$$

                                        To ensure numerical stability against quantile boundary singularity, clip:

                                        $$U_{m, h, s} \leftarrow \min\left( \max(U_{m, h, s}, \, 10^{-6}), \, 1 - 10^{-6} \right)$$

                                        1. Step 3 — Non-Parametric Empirical Marginal Inversion:

                                          2. Let $\mathbf{e}_s = [e_{s, 1}, e_{s, 2}, \dots, e_{s, M}]^T$ be the historical empirical residual vector for series $s$.

                                          The empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) $\hat{F}_s$ and its generalized inverse (empirical quantile function) $\hat{F}_s^{-1}(u)$ are defined as:

                                          $$\hat{F}_s^{-1}(u) = \inf \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} : \hat{F}_s(x) \ge u \right\}$$

                                          We map uniform sample $U_{m, h, s}$ through the empirical quantile function of series $s$:

                                          $$\epsilon_{m, h, s} = \hat{F}_s^{-1}\left( U_{m, h, s} \right) = \text{Quantile}\left( \mathbf{e}_s, \, U_{m, h, s} \right)$$

                                          Method 4: Joint Empirical Resampling (method="empirical")

                                          Direct joint non-parametric bootstrap from the empirical error matrix $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ via row resampling with replacement.

                                          Prediction Intervals and Quantiles API

                                          Across all classes (prob_forecasts, mv_prob_forecasts, multi_prob_forecasts), uncertainty intervals are accessed through two unified methods:

                                          1. sample_quantiles(quantiles=[0.05, 0.5, 0.95])

                                          Computes arbitrary quantiles from simulated sample paths $\left\{ \tilde{Y}_{m, h, s} \right\}_{m=1}^B$:

                                          $$\hat{q}_{\alpha}(h, s) = \text{Quantile}\left( \left\{ \tilde{Y}_{m, h, s} \right\}_{m=1}^B, \, \alpha \right)$$

                                          2. prediction_intervals(coverage=0.90, conformal=False)

                                          Sample-Based Intervals (conformal=False, default)

                                          Computes symmetric empirical bounds for coverage level $(1 - \gamma)$ (e.g. $\gamma = 0.10$ for 90% coverage):

                                          $$\text{Lower}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) = \hat{q}_{\gamma / 2}(h, s)$$

                                          $$\text{Upper}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) = \hat{q}_{1 - \gamma / 2}(h, s)$$

                                          $$\hat{I}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) = \left[ \text{Lower}_{1-\gamma}(h, s), \, \text{Upper}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) \right]$$

                                          Distribution-Free Conformal Prediction Intervals (conformal=True)

                                          Uses nonconformity scores $\alpha_{h, s} = |e_{h, s}|$ stored in self.non_conform:

                                          $$\hat{q}_{\text{conf}}(h, s) = \text{Quantile}\left( \text{non\_conform}_{h, s}, \, \left\lceil (1 - \gamma)(M + 1) \right\rceil / M \right)$$

                                          $$\text{Lower}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) = \hat{y}_{h, s} - \hat{q}_{\text{conf}}(h, s)$$

                                          $$\text{Upper}_{1-\gamma}(h, s) = \hat{y}_{h, s} + \hat{q}_{\text{conf}}(h, s)$$

                                          Guarantees finite-sample coverage without distributional assumptions.
